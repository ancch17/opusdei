How do you stay rooted
when the world will not
stop moving?

We are pushed to adapt, to perform, to keep up. Somewhere in all of it, living your faith on purpose gets harder than it should be. On Friday evening we are going to sit down and talk about that honestly — for two hours, with the microphone open.

Who you'll be listening to

Two people, one microphone, and your questions

We are delighted to welcome Monsignor Mariano Fazio, Auxiliary Vicar of Opus Dei, to Singapore. Argentine by birth, a historian by training and a philosopher by doctorate, he spent six years as Rector of the Pontifical University of the Holy Cross in Rome and has written more than twenty books on faith in a secular age. He has been thinking about this exact question — how a Christian lives in a world that has stopped waiting for him — for most of his working life.

He will be in conversation with Sharon Tong, formerly of Channel NewsAsia. That matters: a broadcast journalist's instinct is to keep ideas tethered to the actual week people are having. Expect the abstract to be pulled back down to earth, repeatedly.

Then the floor opens. There is real time set aside for audience questions — this is a conversation, not a lecture with a polite five minutes at the end.

Whether you are a student, a young professional, someone deep into a career, or simply after fresh air in your Christian life — you are welcome. Bring someone.

It is in the midst of the most material things of the earth that we must sanctify ourselves, serving God and all mankind. St Josemaría Escrivá, founder of Opus Dei

Why we're asking

There is no ticket. There is still a bill.

Nobody pays at the door on Friday, and that is deliberate. The moment an evening like this has a price on it, the people who most need to be in the room start doing sums in their head about whether they can justify coming. We would rather they just came.

But free to attend has never meant free to hold. There is a room to fill and pay for. Sound that has to carry to the back row. Something to drink afterwards, when the conversations that actually change people tend to happen. And a speaker who has travelled a very long way to spend two hours with a room full of Singaporeans he has never met.

All of it is covered by people who will be sitting in that room beside you — quietly, without being asked twice, so that the person in the next seat never has to think about it.

If Friday is going to cost you nothing, would you consider covering somebody else's seat?
